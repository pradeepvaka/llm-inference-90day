Tier 0 · Dev

One GPU, everything local

Your workstation or a single cloud GPU. Docker Compose: one vLLM container, Prometheus, Grafana. Purpose: the benchmark harness and kernel labs run here.

  • 1× GPU, 24–80 GB (4090 / L4 / A100 / H100)
  • Models: 7–8B fp16, or 32–70B quantized
  • Good for: correctness, profiling, writeups
Tier 1 · Portfolio

Two–four GPUs, real scheduling

The tier you publish numbers from. One node serves, one node generates load — never benchmark a server from itself. Kubernetes or plain systemd + Compose both count; the runbook matters more than the orchestrator.

  • Server node + separate load-gen node
  • Gateway in front (Stage 11 patterns)
  • Good for: TTFT/TPOT under load, preemption, chunked prefill
Tier 2 · Burst

Cloud burst, same harness

Rent H100s by the hour to validate that your Tier-1 conclusions scale. The harness, configs, and report format stay identical — only the GPU line in the report changes. Tear down when the run ends; cost discipline is part of the portfolio.

  • Spot/on-demand H100 or A100-80GB
  • Same YAML configs, same seeds
  • Good for: 70B, tensor parallelism, $/M at scale

What every node runs

  • Engine: vLLM (or SGLang) pinned to an exact version — record it in every report; numbers rot as software changes.
  • Weights: local NVMe cache, checksummed. A 70B fp16 download is 140 GB — you do not pull it per deploy.
  • Metrics: engine metrics endpoint → Prometheus. The four panels that matter: TTFT p99, TPOT p99, running/waiting requests, KV cache utilization.
  • Logs: one JSON line per request — queue wait, prefill ms, decode ms, tokens in/out (your Day 16 log format, now permanent).
  • Health: /health + a synthetic canary request every minute; a GPU that answers pings but not tokens is down.

The sizing math you publish with it

Every cluster README earns trust by showing its capacity arithmetic, from Days 8–10:

KV bytes/token = 2 (K,V) × layers × KV heads × head_dim × dtype bytes 8B fp16: 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 131,072 B = 128 KB/token Usable KV memory = HBM − weights − activations − overhead Max sequences ≈ usable KV ÷ (128 KB × context length)

Worked check, 8B on one 80 GB H100: 16 GB weights leaves ~60 GB for KV → at 4k context (512 MB/sequence) that's roughly 115 concurrent sequences before preemption starts — and your benchmark should show exactly where that wall appears.

Whiteboard defense: for each choice in this stack you should be able to say why — vLLM vs SGLang (scheduler + ecosystem vs radix-tree prefix caching), Compose vs Kubernetes (operational surface you can actually maintain), fp16 vs FP8 weights (accuracy evidence from Stage 6, not vibes). If you can't defend it, simplify it until you can.

Publish benchmarks people can trust

Day 82's methodology, made permanent. A benchmark is a claim plus its evidence: fixed setup, one variable at a time, percentiles instead of averages, and the exact commands to reproduce it.

Fix, vary, report

  • Fix: model + quantization + engine version, GPU type and count, prompt/output length distributions, arrival process, warmup, measurement window.
  • Vary: one thing — concurrency, chunk size, quantization, engine. A table where three things changed proves nothing.
  • Report: TTFT and TPOT at p50 and p99, sustained tok/s, goodput under your SLO, $/M tokens, and the repro command.

Goodput is the honest headline: throughput subject to an SLO (e.g. TPOT < 100 ms). Peak tok/s at a 2-second TPOT is a number no chat user would accept.

The seven sins — audit others, never commit them

  • Peak instead of sustained throughput
  • Tiny outputs (prefill theater inflates tok/s)
  • No arrival process — closed-loop concurrency hides queueing
  • Warmup runs counted in the results
  • Cherry-picked batch size
  • $/M mixing on-demand and reserved pricing
  • One run, no variance

Reading a vendor's “10,000 tok/s on 8B!”? Ask three questions: what output length, what arrival process, and what p99 TPOT?

Cost benchmarks: dollars per million tokens

The unit economics that decide build-vs-buy. One formula, applied honestly, with utilization doing most of the damage.

$/M output tokens = (GPU $/hr × GPU count) ÷ (sustained tok/s × 3,600 s/hr) × 1,000,000 Effective $/M = headline $/M ÷ utilization ← a GPU busy 30% of the day costs 3.3× its headline rate

Self-host wins when…

Utilization is high and steady (batch jobs, internal tools with queue depth), the model is open-weight, and someone — you — owns the pager. The fixed cost amortizes across every token, all day.

APIs win when…

Traffic is spiky or tiny, the model must be frontier-class, or the team's scarce hours are worth more than the GPU delta. You are renting elasticity and an ops team, not just tokens.

The trap

Comparing your peak tok/s against an API's list price. Compare sustained tok/s at your SLO, at your measured utilization, including the load-gen and idle hours. Most “self-hosting is 10× cheaper” posts fail exactly here — sin #1 and sin #6 together.

Publish both numbers: headline $/M at saturation and effective $/M at your real duty cycle. The pair is more credible than either alone — and it's the analysis a platform team actually hires for.

Kernel optimization writeups

Stage 8 gave you the kernels. A writeup turns one kernel into evidence of method: a baseline you measured, a roofline that predicted the headroom, one optimization at a time, and an honest end-to-end effect — including when it's small.

The six-part writeup

  • 1 · Problem & baseline. What the kernel does, where it sits in decode, its measured time and % of step — from a profiler trace, not a guess.
  • 2 · Roofline. Bytes moved, FLOPs, arithmetic intensity, % of peak bandwidth achieved. The roofline says how much headroom exists before you write a line of CUDA/Triton.
  • 3 · One optimization. Fusion, tiling, vectorized loads, warp specialization — one idea, explained with the memory-traffic math.
  • 4 · Correctness. allclose against the PyTorch reference, tolerance stated, edge shapes tested.
  • 5 · Results. Before/after table (mean ± std, 3+ runs), % of peak achieved, and the end-to-end tok/s effect.
  • 6 · Limitations & what's next. Shapes where it loses, hardware it assumes, the next bottleneck in line.

Why the end-to-end number is mandatory

Amdahl's law is the writeup's conscience. In 8B decode at batch 1, every major op is bandwidth- or latency-bound and the whole step is ≈4.8 ms on H100:

MLP projections
~52%
QKV + O projections
~26%
Attention (2k ctx)
~10%
Norms, softmax, sampler
~12%

A 2× faster RMSNorm that is 2% of the step buys you ~1% end-to-end. Say so in the writeup. Reviewers and hiring managers trust the author who reports the 1% — and the kernel that is 26% of the step gets their full attention.

Fused RMSNorm — the teaching kernel

Problem. Naive RMSNorm in PyTorch launches several elementwise kernels, each re-reading the row from HBM. For d=4096 the math is trivial (~8k FLOP/row); the op is pure memory traffic plus launch overhead — and decode launches it 2× per layer per token.

Optimization. One Triton kernel: one program per row, load once into SRAM/registers, compute sum-of-squares, normalize, scale by γ, store once. Traffic drops to the theoretical minimum: one read, one write.

Benchmark protocol (from Stage 8). Rows ∈ {128, 2048, 8192}, d=4096, fp16; report achieved GB/s and % of the GPU's peak bandwidth for fused vs torch.nn.functional.rms_norm, 3 runs each.

The writeup's real payload is the method: profiler first, roofline second, kernel third. This is the kernel every inference engineer writes once; yours is the one with the receipts.

FlashAttention — tiling attention into SRAM

Problem. Materializing the n×n score matrix costs 1.07 GB per layer at n=4096, 32 heads, fp16 (Day 3's arithmetic) — attention becomes an HBM-traffic problem long before it's a math problem.

Optimization. Tile Q/K/V into SRAM-sized blocks; maintain a running softmax (max + sum rescaling — the “online softmax”) so the full score matrix never exists in HBM. Same math, O(n) memory traffic instead of O(n²) writes.

Benchmark protocol. n ∈ {512, 2048, 8192}; your Triton kernel vs F.scaled_dot_product_attention and, where available, flash_attn_func; report time, implied TFLOP/s, and HBM traffic saved.

Metrics to fill from your run

Score-matrix bytes avoided (n=4096)≈1.07 GB / layer
Key identity to verifyOnline-softmax output ≡ naive, allclose
Where it winsPrefill & long context — compute-dense
Where it barely helpsBatch-1 decode — seq=1 query, already tiny

That last line is the mark of a serious writeup: FlashAttention is a prefill/long-context optimization. Claiming decode wins from it would be sin-adjacent.

Fused dequant GEMV — attacking the 4.8 ms

Problem. Batch-1 decode is a sequence of GEMVs streaming all weights per token. With INT4 weights, a naive path dequantizes into a temporary fp16 buffer — writing and re-reading gigabytes to save gigabytes.

Optimization. Fuse dequantization into the GEMV: load packed INT4, unpack and scale in registers, multiply-accumulate, never materialize the fp16 weights. Traffic approaches the INT4 ideal: 0.5 bytes/param — a theoretical ~4× decode speedup over fp16 streaming, bounded by everything else in the step.

Benchmark protocol. Per-projection timing (QKV, O, MLP) at 8B shapes; end-to-end TPOT vs the unfused path; accuracy spot-check (perplexity delta from Stage 6) alongside the speed numbers.

Metrics to fill from your run

Weight traffic / token, 8B INT4≈4 GB vs 16 GB fp16
Theoretical TPOT floor (H100)≈1.2 ms vs 4.8 ms fp16
Reality discountAttention, norms, launch overhead don't shrink

Speed without the accuracy line is marketing. The Stage 6 discipline — every compression claim ships with its eval — is what separates a writeup from an ad.

Check yourself

Sampled from the Day 90 gauntlet. Answer cold; the re-derivation is the learning.

1 · A vendor claims 10,000 tok/s on an 8B model. Your first three questions?

What output length? (Short outputs make it prefill theater.) What arrival process / concurrency? (Closed-loop at 256 hides queueing.) What p99 TPOT? (Probably seconds — unusable for chat.) Bonus: sustained or peak, and how many runs?

2 · Your cluster: 8B fp16 on one 80 GB H100, 4k context. Roughly when does preemption start, and how do you know?

KV per sequence = 128 KB/token × 4,096 ≈ 512 MB. With ~60 GB usable for KV after 16 GB of weights and overhead, that's ≈115 concurrent sequences. You know because the running/waiting gauges and KV-utilization panel in your dashboard show it — and your benchmark's knee lands there.

3 · Your fused kernel is 2× faster. The PR reviewer asks “so what?” — what do you answer?

With Amdahl: the kernel's share of the decode step (from the profiler), the resulting end-to-end TPOT/tok/s delta measured, and the shapes where it doesn't help. “2× on 2% of the step ≈ 1% end-to-end” said plainly earns more trust than any headline.

4 · Headline $/M is $0.40 but your GPU is busy 25% of the day. What do you publish?

Both numbers: $0.40/M at saturation and ≈$1.60/M effective at your 25% duty cycle — with the utilization measurement shown. The pair is the analysis; either alone is a sales pitch.

5 · Why is “PR merged” not one of the capstone acceptance criteria?

Because merge timing belongs to the maintainers, not you. The criteria you control: PR opened, CI green, before/after benchmarks in the description, tests added, round-1 feedback addressed. Judge yourself on those; shepherd the merge with same-day responses.

6 · Two numbers you'll never forget?

128 KB of KV cache per token for Llama-3.1-8B fp16 — and the H100 ridge point, ≈295 FLOP/byte. The first sizes every serving system you'll ever build; the second tells you which side of the roofline you're on.
